Overview

Opening a narrowed heart artery

Coronary angioplasty is a procedure used to open clogged heart arteries. A tiny balloon is temporarily inserted and inflated where the artery is clogged to help widen it.

It is often combined with a stent: a small wire mesh tube placed permanently to prop the artery open and reduce its chance of narrowing again. Some stents are coated with medication to help keep the artery open (drug-eluting stents), while others are not (bare-metal stents).

Angioplasty can improve symptoms of blocked arteries, such as chest pain and shortness of breath. It can also be used during a heart attack to quickly open a blocked artery and reduce damage to the heart.

Why is it done?

Treating atherosclerosis

Angioplasty treats atherosclerosis: the slow build-up of fatty plaques in your heart's blood vessels. Your cardiologist may suggest it when medication or lifestyle changes aren't enough, or if you have a heart attack, worsening chest pain (angina) or other symptoms.

Angioplasty isn't right for everyone. If the main artery to the left side of your heart is narrowed, your heart muscle is weak, or several vessels are diseased, coronary artery bypass surgery may be a better option. This also applies to some people with diabetes and multiple blockages. Your cardiologist will talk you through it.

Risks

Understanding the risks

Angioplasty is less invasive than bypass surgery, but it still carries some risk. The most common is re-narrowing of the artery (restenosis), which stents were developed to reduce.

  • Blood clots. Clots can form within stents after the procedure. Take your blood-thinning medication exactly as prescribed.
  • Bleeding. Usually a bruise where the catheter went in. Serious bleeding is uncommon.
  • Rare risks. Heart attack, coronary artery damage, kidney problems from the contrast dye, stroke and abnormal heart rhythms.
How to prepare

Before your procedure

Your cardiologist will review your medical history and examine you. You'll have a coronary angiogram, an imaging test using contrast dye, to see whether your blockages can be treated with angioplasty. If a blockage is found, angioplasty may be done straight after the angiogram.

You'll usually stop eating and drinking 6–8 hours beforehand. Routine tests may include a chest X-ray, ECG and blood tests. The night before:

  • Follow your doctor's instructions about adjusting your current medications
  • Gather all your medications to take to hospital, including nitroglycerin if you take it
  • Take approved medications with only small sips of water
  • Arrange transport home. You won't be able to drive the next day
What you can expect

On the day

The procedure is performed in a cardiac catheterisation lab by a cardiologist and a specialised team. It's done through an artery in your groin, arm or wrist, and can take up to several hours depending on the number of blockages.

  1. Numbing and accessThe area is cleaned and numbed. A small needle is used to access an artery in your leg or arm.
  2. Guiding the catheterA thin guide wire and catheter are threaded through the artery to the blockage. You may feel pressure but shouldn't feel sharp pain.
  3. Imaging the blockageA small amount of dye is injected so the blockage shows on X-ray images called angiograms.
  4. Widening the arteryA small balloon at the tip of the catheter is inflated to widen the artery, sometimes several times.
  5. Placing the stentThe collapsed stent is guided to the blockage on a balloon, expanded into place, and stays there permanently.
After the procedure

Recovery

After a planned procedure, you'll usually stay in hospital for one day while your heart is monitored and your medications are adjusted. Most people return to work or their normal routine the following week. At home, drink plenty of fluids and avoid strenuous exercise or heavy lifting for at least a day.

Call your doctor straight away if the catheter site bleeds, swells or becomes painful, you notice signs of infection, the limb changes colour or temperature, you feel faint or weak, or you develop chest pain or shortness of breath.

Most people who have had angioplasty will need to take aspirin indefinitely. After a stent, a blood thinner such as clopidogrel is usually needed for a year or longer. Talk to your cardiologist before stopping any of these medications.

Results

Better blood flow, and a healthy heart from here

Angioplasty greatly increases blood flow through the narrowed artery. Chest pain generally eases and you may be able to exercise more. It doesn't make heart disease go away, so healthy habits still matter:

  • Quit smoking
  • Lower your cholesterol
  • Maintain a healthy weight
  • Control conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure
  • Get regular exercise

If you have chest pain at rest, or pain that doesn't respond to nitroglycerin, call 000.
